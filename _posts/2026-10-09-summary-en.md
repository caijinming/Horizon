### [htmx Creator&\#x27;s &\#x27;Yes, and&\#x27; Essay Defends Learning to Code in the AI Era](https://htmx.org/essays/yes-and/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

The creator of htmx has published an essay titled &quot;Yes, and&quot; on htmx.org arguing that learning to program remains worthwhile despite recent advances in AI coding tools, addressed to students deciding whether to study computer science. In the Hacker News discussion \(219 points, 75 comments at the time of analysis\), the author — posting as recursivedoubts — confirmed he wrote the piece for prospective CS majors, citing his own son&\#x27;s just-begun CS degree, and said he still stands by its argument despite being &quot;startled&quot; by recent AI progress. The essay engages the question of whether the shift from coding to prompting parallels the earlier transition from assembly language to high-level languages, an analogy several commenters rejected because prompting lacks the formal predictability that compilers provide.

hackernews · Michelangelo11 · Oct 8, 09:48 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50003796)

**「Background」** Carson Gross, the creator of htmx and a computer science professor at Montana State University, wrote the essay as advice for young people interested in computer science who are worried about their future given rapid advances in AI. He frames programming as fundamentally two things: problem-solving using computers and learning to control complexity while solving those problems. The piece also engages a claim now common in developer discussions — that prompting AI models will make hand-written coding obsolete the way high-level languages replaced assembly — which is the analogy at the center of the debate the essay has drawn.

**「What it means for students and developers」** For students and early-career developers, the thread&\#x27;s convergent practical point is that strong fundamentals appear to be what makes AI-assisted development effective rather than redundant: the essay&\#x27;s author reported that &quot;the most effective vibe coders are already excellent developers,&quot; and one commenter described reaching production-quality results only through meticulous guidance plus heavy time and token spending on testing and verification. The actionable takeaway is to treat prompting skill as a complement to engineering fundamentals and verification practices — though this rests on one author&\#x27;s argument and commenters&\#x27; anecdotes, not measured outcomes.

**「How Hacker News responded」** Commenters split on the assembly analogy: layer8 argued the comparison fails because compilers let you predict exactly how source changes alter a compiled program&\#x27;s behavior &quot;with formal precision,&quot; a guarantee prompting does not offer. Pushing in the other direction, gregwebs reported that AI &quot;used properly&quot; — with meticulous guidance and extensive testing — is now &quot;a better programmer than I am,&quot; though he observed that very few people currently use it that way.

### [ThinkingBox benchmark grades agent reliability on 507 stateful workflows across 20 repeated runs](https://www.reddit.com/r/MachineLearning/comments/1x17shf/thinkingbox_solving_an_agent_task_once_vs_solving/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Microsoft-affiliated researchers released Thinkingbox-Bench, a public benchmark of 507 policy-conditioned business workflows across five domains \(retail, travel/hospitality, auto insurance, neobank internal IT, and consulting IT/HR\), with the paper, code, dataset, and a Hugging Face OpenEnv environment available. Each task runs 20 independent attempts from an identical clean backend \(10,140 trials per model\), a simulated user holds private context and reveals it only when asked, and grading compares terminal backend state and side effects against the required end state, with wrong, missing, or extra effects failing; 477 of 507 tasks are graded on state alone, and 30 also check a narrow property of the final response. The benchmark reports pass@1, pass@20 \(tasks solved at least once\), and all-20 \(solved on every attempt, an observed count on a fixed trial budget rather than an estimator\), and these metrics reorder nine measured models nearly in reverse: Kimi-K3 solved 93.89% of tasks at least once but only 13.41% on all 20 attempts, Claude Opus 5 discovered fewer tasks \(79.09%\) yet repeated far more reliably \(47.53%\), and Qwen3.8-27B posted 89.35% versus 7.50%. In a retrospective ablation of 121,680 valid trials across 12 models, 67.24% of the 79,853 failed attempts still terminated cleanly without a final tool error—so a completion-style proxy would have scored them as done—while state checks attributed failures to wrong field values \(77.61%\), unintended extra effects \(43.30%\), and missing required effects \(25.36%, with overlapping categories\); the authors note the tasks are synthetic reconstructions of enterprise workflow patterns rather than production traffic, and raw evaluation trajectories are not released.

reddit · r/MachineLearning · /u/tuhin\_k · Oct 9, 00:50

**「Background」** Agent evaluations have conventionally emphasized one-shot success \(pass@1\), and a run is often counted as complete when the model&\#x27;s final answer looks right — without checking whether the backend system was actually left in the required state. The paper behind this release, &quot;One Success Isn&\#x27;t Reliability,&quot; frames the complementary gap as discovery versus repeatability: a model that solves a task at least once is not necessarily one that solves it every time. Thinkingbox-Bench addresses both issues by grading 507 policy-conditioned workflows — spanning retail, hospitality, auto insurance, neobank internal IT, and consulting IT/HR support — on terminal state, with the paper and dataset publicly available on arXiv and Hugging Face.

**「What this means for agent evaluators」** Teams evaluating agents for stateful backend workflows can now run all 507 tasks against their own models through the Hugging Face OpenEnv environment, where each finished episode returns a binary pass/fail reward; the framework is MIT-licensed and the benchmark data ships under CDLA-Permissive-2.0. The concrete consequence for rankings is that single-attempt success does not survive repetition: Kimi-K3 solved 93.89% of tasks at least once but only 13.41% on all 20 attempts, while Claude Opus 5 discovered fewer tasks yet repeated at 47.53%, nearly inverting the pass@20 leaderboard. Developers building reliability leaderboards or choosing agents for database-touching workflows should therefore weight the all-attempt success rate over discovery metrics, and report both the observed all-20 count and the plug-in pass-k estimate since they answer different questions and can differ substantially.

### [Anthropic Launches Opt-In AI Vulnerability Scanner for Open Source Projects](https://www.anthropic.com/research/launching-opt-in-vuln-finding-service-for-open-source) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Anthropic has launched OSS Scanner, a free, opt-in vulnerability scanning service for eligible open source projects, with core maintainers able to apply by submitting a GitHub PR. Reports are generated by Claude models without human review and include reproduction details, vulnerability explanations, and patch suggestions where possible; Anthropic explicitly notes the reports may contain errors. The company says the effort surfaced more than 29,000 candidate vulnerabilities over the past six months, of which about 6,000 were manually reviewed, and that 85 of 97 high or critical vulnerabilities from early testing met its disclosure process requirements. These figures are self-reported by Anthropic and have not been independently verified.

telegram · zaihuapd · Oct 9, 02:00

**「Background」** Anthropic presents OSS Scanner as informed by Project Glasswing, its earlier program that used Claude models to find vulnerabilities in software, making this a continuation of that work rather than a first attempt at AI-driven vuln discovery. The service follows the coordinated disclosure practice common in open source security, in which candidate vulnerabilities are reported to project maintainers for validation and remediation rather than published outright.

**「Why it matters」** Core maintainers of eligible open source projects can apply for the free service via a GitHub PR and may receive automated vulnerability reports with reproduction steps and patch suggestions. Because Anthropic does not human-review the reports and acknowledges they may be wrong, maintainers should independently verify findings before merging suggested patches or acting on a report.
